# 5-minute demo script (video: `submission/02_Hackathon_Explainer_Video/VoltSight_Hackathon_Explainer.mp4`, 4:48)

The video is a recording of the real VoltSight console (live profile, `deploy/live/up.sh`) with synthetic neural-TTS narration (Piper, `en_US-ryan-high`); slides are used only for the problem, architecture, engineering and matrix scenes. Browser scenes were recorded with `tests/e2e/record_submission.mjs` (Playwright against the running stack); nothing is mocked. Sign-in was done before recording; no credentials appear on screen.

| Time | Screen | Mouse action | Voice-over | Technical point | Judge takeaway |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Dashboard (live) | Slow pass over the five summary cards | At one hundred thousand connected vehicles, a one second stream becomes one hundred thousand decisions every second. VoltSight turns that telemetry into a live range risk and charging system. It tells fleet operators which electric vehicles are at risk, why they are at risk, and where they can charge. What you see is the real application, running on synthetic fleet data. | Real application, synthetic data; 100K target scale | The product is real and live |
| 0:21 | Problem slide | — | The problem. The range on the dashboard is not usable range. It depends on the vehicle, the route, temperature, load and battery age, and it changes the moment a charger fails. Charging at the wrong time is expensive, battery health is invisible, and at fleet scale an answer is only useful if it arrives in seconds. The official challenge asks for a hundred thousand vehicles and more than a hundred thousand events per second. | Range uncertainty, charger outages, cost, latency; official scale | Why range-risk matters |
| 0:47 | Architecture slide (Figure 1) | — | Here is the architecture. A Go simulator streams telemetry to a gateway over mutual T L S. Events land in Kafka, keyed by vehicle. A stream worker removes duplicates, tracks vehicle state, and runs the range risk engine, which looks up the distance to the nearest available charger from a graph overlay. Alerts return through Kafka to an alert service that writes to PostgreSQL and publishes to Redis. ClickHouse keeps the history. The A P I serves the React console with J S O N and server sent events, and Keycloak handles identity. The stores differ because the access patterns differ: PostgreSQL for transactions and tenant isolation, Redis for hot state, ClickHouse for analytics. On the same host, ClickHouse ingested about four times faster than PostgreSQL. | Gateway→Kafka→worker/risk engine→stores→API→console; polyglot persistence rationale (ClickHouse ≈4.2× PostgreSQL insert) | Engineering is distributed, not a dashboard on one DB |
| 1:35 | Dashboard cards and live alert feed | Hover each card; move over the alert list | This is the live dashboard. The tenant has forty thousand registered vehicles, and eight hundred are reporting live right now. The cards show vehicles moving or charging, average state of charge, critical and warning alerts, and the charger network. In this demo scenario the simulator starts some vehicles at low charge and, five minutes in, takes most chargers out of service, so alerts appear through the real pipeline, not inserted into the database. | Deterministic demo scenario: low-SoC vehicles, 99% charger outage at +300 s; alerts come from the risk engine | Alerts are produced by the pipeline |
| 2:02 | Live map | Click Bengaluru, Surat, All cities, Chennai; scroll-zoom | The map shows the fleet. Markers and clusters come from the backend, coloured by risk: green is normal, orange is low range, red is critical, and blue are chargers. The city buttons fly to Chennai, Bengaluru or Surat and filter on the server. One honest note: the basemap tiles come from Open Free Map, which this recording environment could not reach, so the app shows its markers only fallback. On a normal connection the same map draws roads and labels. | Server-side city filter; markers/clusters from /v1/map/*; basemap fallback explained honestly | Spatial risk concentration |
| 2:30 | Alert evidence | Click an alert in the feed (route drawn on map), then open the Alerts page and select a row | Open an alert. This is the key operational decision. It shows the state of charge, the estimated range, the range left after a safety margin, the nearest available charger, and the route to it, computed with A star over the road graph. The decision itself is a constant time lookup into a shortest path overlay that updates incrementally when a charger fails. | SoC, EWMA range, safety margin, nearest available charger, A* route; O(1) overlay lookup | Why a vehicle is at risk and where it can charge |
| 2:52 | Copilot | Click the example question, wait for the answer | The Charge Ops Copilot answers from tools, not from memory. Ask which vehicles are at risk. It calls typed, allow listed tools. The tenant comes from the authenticated token, never from the model. Every answer cites alert identifiers or says insufficient evidence. Any write, such as a charging plan, is only proposed: a human approves it, and every step is audited. In this build the default provider is a deterministic stub, so no external language model produced this answer. | Typed allow-listed tools, tenant from JWT, citations, human approval, audit; stub provider stated | AI with guardrails |
| 3:21 | Engineering montage slide | — | Under the interface, this is a distributed streaming platform. Prometheus scrapes the gateway and the worker. SQL tuning was measured with real query plans: a deep alert page fell from one point three seconds to a fraction of a millisecond with keyset pagination and a partial index. Security includes row level security, role based access, and an OWASP ZAP baseline with fifty eight checks passed, zero failures, and three documented warning groups. | Prometheus targets; EXPLAIN ANALYZE before/after; ZAP 58/0/3 | Measured engineering depth |
| 3:49 | Requirement matrix slide | — | Every requirement is traced to evidence, with honest statuses. Of sixty requirements, twenty eight pass, twenty eight are partial, two are blocked and two fail. Measured: ninety eight point three thousand events per second for sixty seconds with a hundred thousand vehicles; zero events lost in a three times burst and five chaos scenarios; A P I ninety fifth percentile latency of twenty seven milliseconds. The five second critical alert target was not met at that load on one host, and soak, scale out and production cloud deployment were not run. | 28/28/2/2; 98.3K ev/s, 0 lost, API p95 26.8 ms; alert target FAIL | Honest status |
